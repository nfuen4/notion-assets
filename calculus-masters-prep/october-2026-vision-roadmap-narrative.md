## 0. Where this sits

The full program is 24 weeks and ends with the derivative. This document covers only the
next seven weeks — Week 2 through Week 8 of the master schedule — because that block has a
clean edge: it ends on **Sunday 1 November** with the Chapter 2 Mastery Test, which is
**Major Phase Gate 2**.

That is not an arbitrary stopping point. It is the moment the program crosses from
*fundamentals* into *functions*, and it is the last date at which a foundational crack can
be repaired cheaply.

# Part 1 — Vision

## 1.1 The near-horizon vision statement

> By 1 November I will have closed Chapters P, 1 and 2 behind three closed-book tests at
> 80% or better. Not because the material got easier, but because I showed up for 55
> minutes a day and wrote down every mistake I made. October is not the month I become a
> math person. October is the month the evidence gets too heavy to argue with.

**Lock-screen anchor for the window:** *Three chapters. Three tests. Forty-four days.*

## 1.2 The product vision statement

**For** a career-changer targeting a quantitative master's program,
**who** is blocked by an unverified algebra foundation rather than by ability or ambition,
**the Calculus Masters Prep program** is a self-directed quantitative curriculum
**that** converts 55 minutes a day into audited, closed-book mastery from real numbers
through the derivative,
**unlike** video courses and tutorial binges that produce familiarity without recall,
**it** gates every chapter behind an 80% closed-book test and classifies every error by root
cause, so fragility surfaces in October rather than in a graduate classroom.

## 1.3 What "done" looks like on 1 November

- Chapter P, Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 each passed closed-book at ≥80%.
- The Formula & Proof Notebook holds hand-written entries for every rule met in the window.
- The defect log shows the error mix shifting from conceptual to mechanical. That shift, not
  the test score, is the real signal.
- Learning media exists **ahead of** each study week, never behind it.
- The difference quotient can be written from memory and explained geometrically as the
  slope of a secant line.

## 1.4 Anti-goals — what this window is explicitly not for

- **Not** getting ahead into Chapter 3. Trigonometry is November's problem.
- **Not** building the perfect media pipeline. The automation exists to serve the math; the
  moment it costs study time it has failed.
- **Not** producing a visual for every section. Most sections need reps, not artwork.
- **Not** recovering missed days by doubling up. Slippage is absorbed into the buffer day.

## 2.3 The 5 October pace cliff

This is the most important finding in the plan, and it comes straight out of the master
schedule.

| Chapter | Sections | Weeks | Sections per week | Sessions per section |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 9 | 4 | 2.3 | ~2.7 |
| 1 | 10 | 2 | 5.0 | ~1.2 |
| 2 | 8 | 2 | 4.0 | ~1.5 |

**The workload roughly doubles on 5 October.** Chapter P gets nearly three 55-minute
sessions per section. Chapter 1 gets barely one. Nothing in the daily engine changes, so the
schedule silently assumes Chapter 1 is half as hard as Chapter P. It is not — 1.3, 1.6 and
1.7 are the first genuinely abstract sections in the book.

Three mitigations, to be applied together:

1. **Front-load during Week 4.** Chapter P week 4 is review and test only, with no new
   material. If the review is clean by Thursday, spend days 5 and 6 on 1.1 and 1.2, the two
   lightest sections in Chapter 1. This converts the Chapter 1 crunch from five sections per
   week to four.
2. **Fast-pass the procedural sections.** 1.1, 1.4, 1.9, 1.10, 2.1 and 2.8 are mechanical.
   One session each, drill-heavy, no media production.
3. **Protect the hard four.** 1.3, 1.5, 1.6 and 1.7 get the recovered sessions. If something
   has to slip, it slips from 1.9 or 1.10, never from the difference quotient.

## 2.4 Media production tiering

The production system specifies 3–5 visuals and a deck per section. Applied to all 21
sections in this window that is roughly 80 visuals and 21 decks, which would become a second
full-time project and violate the system's own operating rule. Sections are therefore tiered
by conceptual load, not treated uniformly.

**Tier 1 — full package** (anchor visual + 3 supporting visuals + deck):
P.7 · 1.3 · 1.5 · 1.6 · 1.7 · 2.4 · 2.6 · 2.7

These are the eight sections where a diagram genuinely outperforms prose: equation families
and extraneous roots, the difference quotient, secant slope as average rate of change,
transformation ordering, composite domains, synthetic division, asymptote behaviour, and
sign charts.

**Tier 2 — anchor visual + retrieval questions, no deck:**
P.8 · P.9 · 1.2 · 1.8 · 2.2 · 2.3 · 2.5

**Tier 3 — retrieval card only, no production:**
1.1 · 1.4 · 1.9 · 1.10 · 2.1 · 2.8

| | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sections | 8 | 7 | 6 | 21 |
| Visuals | 32 | 14 | 0 | **46** |
| Decks | 8 | 0 | 0 | **8** |

Spread across six production weeks that is about eight visuals and one deck per week — a
load the n8n pipeline can absorb without eating study time.

## 2.5 Success metrics

| Metric | Target by 1 Nov | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Session adherence | ≥ 90% of planned sessions | The single strongest predictor of the gate scores |
| Gate scores | ≥ 80% on all three tests | The published exit criterion |
| Defect mix | Type C under 10% of logged errors | Conceptual blockers should be rare by Chapter 2 |
| Media lead time | Assets ready ≥ 1 day before the section is studied | Production ahead of study is the whole point |
| Buffer consumption | ≤ 1 buffer day used per week | Regular buffer use means the plan is too tight |
| Formula notebook | One hand-written entry per new rule | The permanent artifact that outlives the project |

**The metric to watch is the defect mix, not the test scores.** A test score says what
happened once. The error taxonomy says what is actually fragile. If Type B rule gaps are
still frequent in Week 8, Chapter 3 will hurt regardless of what the Chapter 2 test said.

## 2.6 Definition of done, per release

A chapter ships only when all five hold:

- [ ] Every section's textbook exercises completed by hand on paper.
- [ ] Closed-book mastery test scored ≥80%, marked honestly.
- [ ] Every logged defect triaged into Type A, B or C with its remediation applied.
- [ ] Formula notebook entries written for every new rule.
- [ ] No Type C conceptual blocker left open at the gate.

# Part 3 — Narrative

## The October Window

There is a version of this story where the interesting part is the derivative. It sits at
the far end of the program, in Chapter 11, in late spring — the thing that makes the whole
project sound worth doing. Limits, continuity, the slope of a curve at a single point. That
is the part that sounds like mathematics.

October is not that. October is real numbers, factoring, interval notation, the domain of a
composite function, and the long division of polynomials. It is the part of the story nobody
tells, because there is nothing to tell. You sit down for 55 minutes. You do the problems by
hand. You write down what you got wrong. You close the book and do it again tomorrow.

And yet the entire program is decided here.

Look at what Chapter 11 actually asks for. To evaluate a limit that comes back as zero over
zero, there are exactly two techniques: factor the expression, or multiply by the conjugate.
Factoring is P.5. Conjugate rationalization is P.3. Both are September and October work. And
the derivative itself — the definition, the thing the entire twenty-four weeks is pointed at
— is the limit of the difference quotient, and the difference quotient is section 1.3, which
lands in the second week of October.

Which means the machinery for the final chapter is taught in the first three. In October you
shake hands with the derivative without anyone introducing you. You will write
`[f(x+h) - f(x)] / h` on paper in Week 5, grind through the algebra, and it will feel like
nothing — like a formula manipulation exercise with an h in it. Eighteen weeks later the
same expression comes back with a limit wrapped around it and a name attached, and the only
question that will matter is whether your hands already know what to do with it.

That is why the Chapter P test on 4 October is the real test of this window, not the Chapter
2 test at the end of it. It is the first closed-book measurement in the entire project, and
it measures the one thing that cannot be patched later. An 85% on Chapter P means the
algebra is load-bearing. A 72% means there is a crack, and every chapter stacked on top of
it — functions, polynomials, logarithms, trigonometry, and eventually limits — will be built
on a crack. The cost of fixing that in October is one week. The cost of finding it in
March is the project.

Then, on 5 October, the work doubles. Chapter P was nine sections over four weeks. Chapter 1
is ten sections over two. Nothing in the daily engine changes — still 55 minutes, still six
days, still the same four blocks — but the material per session roughly doubles, and it does
it at the exact moment the material becomes abstract for the first time. Transformations of
functions. Composite functions and their domains. Inverses. These are not harder
computations than factoring; they are a different kind of thinking, and the schedule gives
them half the time it gave to arithmetic with exponents.

This is the part worth naming honestly in advance, because it is where the old story gets
its opening. The old story does not announce itself on a day when the work goes well. It
waits for the day when the material is genuinely harder and the time is genuinely shorter,
and it offers a clean explanation: *this is the point where it stops working, and it stops
working because of who you are.* That explanation will feel like insight. It will arrive
somewhere around 12 October, in the middle of section 1.6 or 1.7, on a day when the
transformations keep coming out in the wrong order.

It is not insight. It is a scheduling problem with a known cause and three written
mitigations, and the mitigations are in section 2.3 of this document, decided in advance, in
a calm moment, precisely so they do not have to be invented on a hard day.

So the plan for October is not ambitious. It is deliberately, almost boringly small. Three
chapters. Three tests. Forty-four days. Fifty-five minutes at a time, six days a week, with
the seventh held back so that a missed day is an absorbed day rather than a broken streak.
The error log stays honest — not because honesty is virtuous, but because a defect log that
flatters you is worse than no log at all, and the whole method depends on knowing which of
the three kinds of wrong you are.

What actually gets built in these seven weeks is not knowledge of polynomials. It is
evidence. Right now the claim "I can do this" is a decision, made on faith, against a fair
amount of personal history. By 1 November it can be something else: three closed-book scores
above 80%, a notebook of formulas in your own handwriting, and a defect log that shows
conceptual blockers becoming rule gaps and rule gaps becoming careless sign errors — the
documented signature of someone getting good at something.

That is the whole product. Not a feeling about mathematics. A record that is hard to
dispute, built at a rate of 55 minutes a day, in the least glamorous chapters of the book.

One problem at a time. Doors open.

## Appendix A — The daily engine, unchanged

| Block | Duration | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 min | Retrieval warm-up — two problems from yesterday, no notes |
| 2 | 20 min | Concept ingestion — targeted video or textbook section, hand-written margin notes |
| 3 | 25 min | Active problem solving — paper and pencil, hints only at a genuine roadblock |
| 4 | 5 min | Defect logging — classify errors, note tomorrow's starting problem, close the book |

**The 15-minute rule.** Stuck longer than 15 minutes on one problem: stop, ask for a
Socratic hint, then rework it independently.

## Appendix B — Defect taxonomy

| Code | Classification | Root cause | Remediation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Mechanical slip | Sign error, exponent miscalculation, transcription. Concept is understood. | Slow down; write intermediate steps explicitly |
| **B** | Rule gap | Misapplied formula, forgotten factoring pattern, wrong log property | Add to retrieval flashcards; 5 targeted drills |
| **C** | Conceptual blocker | Does not understand what the object *means* geometrically | Stop solving; break down the proof; rewrite the explanation by hand |
